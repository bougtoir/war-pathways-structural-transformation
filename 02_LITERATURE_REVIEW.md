# 02 — Literature Review

## A. Rationalist explanations of war

**Core.** Fearon (1995), *International Organization* 49(3) — the canonical
taxonomy: war between rational unitary actors requires (i) private information
with incentives to misrepresent, (ii) commitment problems (inability to bind
future behavior, Powell 2006, *IO* 60(1)), or (iii) issue indivisibility.

**Causal mechanism.** War is a bargaining failure: both sides would prefer
*some* negotiated settlement to costly fighting, yet the settlement set
collapses because offers cannot be trusted (info), promises cannot be
enforced (commitment), or the pie cannot be split (indivisibility).

**Explanatory boundary.** The framework presumes a single utility function
per side and rationality throughout. It explains *why bargains fail*, not
*whether war can be mobilized*, *who inside the state prefers it*, or
*whether violence persists*. Preventive war (fighting to lock in a favorable
distribution before power shifts) and preemptive war (striking first under
imminent attack) are special cases of commitment problems under Powell's
formalization.

**Bounded rationality boundary.** Rationalist failures are *structural* (no
acceptable settlement exists), whereas misperception-driven wars are
*epistemic* (a settlement exists but actors fail to see it). The two classes
require different interventions: enforcement/information vs. belief
correction. Our framework keeps them distinct — the `rho` parameter (exogenous
bargaining failure) and `sigma` (belief noise) are separate gates.

## B. Political survival and domestic incentives

**Core.** Selectorate theory (Bueno de Mesquita et al., *The Logic of
Political Survival*, MIT Press 2003); BdM & Siverson (1995) APSR 89(4) — war
losers face sharply elevated removal hazard, especially in democracies;
Chiozza & Goemans (2011) — leaders "gamble for resurrection"; diversionary
war literature; Weeks (2014) on authoritarian subtypes.

**Causal mechanism.** Leaders choose policies to maximize tenure, not national
welfare. External conflict is a domestic instrument: it can rally elites,
redistribute goods, or postpone accountability. War costs are *distributed*,
not uniform — leaders who do not bear battlefield or fiscal costs may prefer
wars the state would reject.

**Explanatory boundary.** Explains *who wants war and why*, but treats
mobilization and escalation as downstream technicalities. Comparative statics:
no feedback from violence to institutions. Crucially, it does not assume
democracies are inherently peaceful — Weeks shows authoritarian audience
costs vary, and democracies can initiate when expected victory is high.

**Implication for this framework.** The leader's war-rationality region need
not coincide with the state's. The divergence (our `delta`) is a structural
variable that institutions can alter — the point standard selectorate work
describes but does not treat as *intervenable*.

## C. Security dilemma and international structure

**Core.** Jervis (1978) World Politics 30(2) — offense–defense balance and
distinguishability determine how severe the security dilemma is; Waltz
(1979) — anarchy as permissive structure; critique/extension through Kydd
(2005) on trust-building via costly signals.

**Causal mechanism.** Under anarchy, one state's security procurement is
another's threat. When offense dominates and postures are
indistinguishable, arms races and preemptive incentives follow even among
status-quo actors.

**Explanatory boundary.** A condition, not a decision rule: the security
dilemma raises the *stakes* and *urgency* but does not itself select war.
Jervis (1997), *System Effects*, adds that interventions in interdependent
systems produce nonlinear, unintended consequences — the theoretical license
for treating escalation as a separate nonlinear stage rather than an
extension of initiation.

## D. Political mobilization and war production

**Core.** State-capacity literature (fiscal-military state; Lamborn's *The
Price of Power*; Kiser & Levi on extraction); mobilization logistics;
opportunity cost of military labor; dual-use technology (civilian
applications of military R&D can raise the value of civilian production,
making mobilization effectively more expensive).

**Causal mechanism.** War requires converting civilian capacity into
organized violence. Fiscal depth, administrative reach, legitimacy, and the
civilian return to labor jointly determine whether a *preferred* war is
*feasible*. This is the least theorized stage in mainstream IR — most models
assume mobilization follows automatically from the decision to fight.

**Explanatory boundary.** Treats states as resource-constrained producers,
not as choosing agents. It supplies the constraint, not the preference.
Our framework elevates it to a first-class gate: willingness without
feasibility produces no war.

## E. Complex systems and conflict diffusion

**Core.** Jervis (1997); Cederman (1997/2020), *Emergent Actors in World
Politics*; threshold/cascade models (Granovetter-type, Watts 2002 in the
broader networks literature); conflict contagion research showing clustering
of civil and interstate violence.

**Causal mechanism.** Conflict diffuses through network ties: alliance
commitments, ethnic kin links, economic interdependence (which can transmit
*or* dampen). Cascades have threshold structure — a small seed can trigger
systemic escalation when local thresholds are low and connectivity is high.

**Explanatory boundary.** System-level models rarely connect to actor-level
incentives; they show *that* cascades occur, not *why* any node adopts
conflict. Our network module therefore ties node thresholds to
stabilizing vs. destabilizing edges rather than assuming connectivity =
conflict.

## F. Evolutionary game theory

**Core.** Replicator dynamics and ESS (Maynard Smith); Skyrms (2004), *The
Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure* — cooperative order as an
adaptive equilibrium; Axelrod's tournaments; institutional evolution
literature treating rules as selected strategies.

**Causal mechanism.** Strategies spread by differential success under
adaptation/imitation, *without* requiring forward-looking rationality. A
nonviolent structure is durable only if it resists invasion by aggressive
strategies — local stability (ESS), basin size, and post-shock recovery are
distinct properties.

**Explanatory boundary.** The mapping to interstate politics is contested:
states are few, not a population; "replication" is institutional diffusion
or elite learning at best. We therefore use EGT only as an illustrative
adaptive-selection metaphor and flag it explicitly.

## G. Peace and institutional transformation

**Core.** Democratic peace (Russett & Oneal 2001, Kantian tripod); commercial
/capitalist peace (Gartzke 2007 — development and capital predict peace
better than democracy does); institutional peace via enforcement (Fortna
2004/2008 on peacekeeping); peace systems and conflict transformation
(Galtung; Boulding's *Stable Peace* 1978); war-obsolescence claims (Mueller
1989/1991, *Retreat from Doomsday*; van Creveld 1991; Kaldor's "new wars").

**Causal mechanism.** Three families: (i) preferences change (norms,
audience costs), (ii) opportunity costs change (development, capital,
trade raise the relative return to nonviolence), (iii) enforcement changes
(third parties solve commitment/information problems ex post).

**Explanatory boundary.** Mostly correlational or ex-post enforcement; the
obsolescence literature asserts the outcome (war declining) without a
mechanism specifying *which structural conditions* must hold and whether
they can be reversed. This is the precise gap the present framework targets:
a mechanism-level decomposition of *where* each peace mechanism binds in
the causal pathway, and *when* it fails.

## Cross-tradition synthesis

The traditions are complementary **stage theories**, not competitors:

| Stage | Best-covered by | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|
| Conflict formation | security dilemma, identity politics | treats conflict as exogenous |
| Bargaining failure | Fearon/Powell rationalist theory | ignores actor-split |
| Selection of force | selectorate/survival literature | assumes feasibility |
| Mobilization | fiscal-military state research | rarely linked to preferences |
| Escalation/persistence | steps-to-war, in-war bargaining | weakly formalized |
| System adaptation | EGT, peace systems, system effects | rarely connected upstream |

No tradition simultaneously splits the actor (state/regime/leader/population),
gates the pathway (willing → feasible → escalates), and asks whether the
gates can be durably narrowed. That is the intended contribution space.

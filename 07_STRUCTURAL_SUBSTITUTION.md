# 07 — Structural Substitution Mechanisms

For each mechanism: supporting theory, contrary evidence, necessary
assumptions, unintended consequences, and whether it constitutes durable
transformation or temporary deterrence. "Gate" refers to the stage where
the mechanism binds (S2 bargaining, S3 selection, S4 mobilization,
S5 escalation, S6 adaptation).

## A. Functional substitution — nonviolent alternatives at lower cost

- **Theory:** bargaining range expands when credible alternatives to
  fighting exist (Fearon's settlement set; Kydd's reassurance; mediation
  literature). Parameter: `a`, `α`.
- **Gate:** S2/S3 — raises U_A for all actors.
- **Simulation:** scenario A (a: 0.30→0.65) cuts p_war ≈ 0.025 → 0.014
  and R_leader 9.3% → 4.5% (illustrative).
- **Contrary evidence:** alternative provision can free resources for war
  (aid fungibility); substitution of the *objective* is not substitution
  of the *means* — leaders may still prefer force for survival reasons
  the alternative cannot match.
- **Necessary assumptions:** alternatives must be credible and valued by
  the decision-level actor (the leader), not just the state.
- **Unintended consequences:** buy-off institutions can become tribute
  that rewards threats (Machiavelli's rule; also Danegeld logic).
- **Durability:** conditional — substitutes must be cheaper to maintain
  than coercion across shocks, else reversible.

## B. Opportunity-cost transformation — civilian development raises mobilization cost

- **Theory:** capitalist peace (Gartzke 2007); commercial peace; guns-vs-
  butter economics. Parameter: `o`.
- **Gate:** S4 primarily (M falls as o rises), secondarily S3 via c_state.
- **Simulation:** scenario B (o: 0.30→0.60) cuts p_war ≈ 0.025 → 0.005,
  feasibility 1.00 → 0.75 — binds at mobilization while leader preference
  barely moves (0.11→0.08). *The mechanism's location in the pathway is
  the substantive claim.*
- **Contrary evidence:** rich states mobilize *more* capacity; development
  can fund militaries; resource wealth lowers o's binding effect; the
  correlation may be reverse-causal (peace enabling development).
- **Assumptions:** civilian returns must actually compete with military
  uses (labor/capital mobility constraints).
- **Unintended consequences:** autarkic withdrawal to reduce dependence;
  dual-use leakage (below).
- **Durability:** moderately durable — structural while development
  persists, reversible under economic collapse.

## C. Dual-use conversion — civilian value of military technologies

- **Theory:** spin-off literature; swords-to-ploughshares; raises both a
  and effective o (civilian returns to the same assets).
- **Gate:** S4 (raises the opportunity cost of sequestering assets) and
  indirectly S3.
- **Contrary evidence:** symmetrically strengthens military capability —
  the same technology improves weapons (the strongest objection: C can
  *expand* R_state by lowering war cost). Nuclear power, GPS, the
  internet: every canonical case cut both ways.
- **Assumptions:** civilian applications must dominate the asset's total
  value; export-control/verification environment matters.
- **Unintended consequences:** dual-use ambiguity worsens the security
  dilemma (indistinguishability — Jervis 1978).
- **Durability:** fragile — easily reversed by sequestration in crises.

## D. State–regime incentive alignment — reduce divergence δ

- **Theory:** selectorate theory; accountability institutions (audience
  costs, removal risk lam); Weeks (2014) on constrained autocrats.
- **Gate:** S3 — collapses R_leader toward R_state; our leader-only
  region is the object this mechanism shrinks.
- **Simulation:** scenario C (δ: 0.60→0.20) gives the largest single
  region contraction (R_leader 9.3% → 2.9%) — in the model, alignment is
  the highest-leverage intervention on the selection gate.
- **Contrary evidence:** alignment can go the other way — a population
  that *wants* war aligns leader incentives upward (nationalism, rally
  effects); accountable democracies still start wars they expect to win.
- **Assumptions:** the state region must be smaller than the leader's —
  i.e., the mechanism only helps where R_state ⊂ R_leader.
- **Unintended consequences:** stronger accountability raises gambling
  for resurrection in already-doomed leaders (Chiozza & Goemans: lam can
  *increase* war attraction when removal is imminent regardless).
- **Durability:** institution-dependent; durable only if insulated from
  incumbent manipulation.

## E. Conflict-pathway transformation — redirect, don't eliminate, disagreement

- **Theory:** Galtung's conflict transformation; Fearon & Laitin's
  in-group policing; judicialization of disputes; Senese & Vasquez —
  remove the realpolitik *steps*, not the disputes.
- **Gate:** S2–S5 structure — inserts alternative pathways between
  bargaining failure and escalation.
- **Contrary evidence:** relocation of violence (van Creveld, Kaldor):
  suppressed interstate war reappears as proxy/civil violence; litigation
  channels exist only where jurisdiction is accepted.
- **Assumptions:** alternative pathways must carry real stakes
  (compensation, status), not just process.
- **Unintended consequences:** institutional capture; forum shopping;
  frozen conflicts that decay into sporadic violence.
- **Durability:** potentially durable — this is closest to the framework's
  core thesis (transform the pathway, not the actors).

## F. Structural adaptation — change constraints that generate conflict

- **Theory:** Jervis's system effects; institutional evolution; the full
  framework's stage-6 claim.
- **Gate:** all — meta-mechanism altering θ itself over time.
- **Contrary evidence:** strategic adaptation by adversaries (arms-race
  responses; exploitation of open institutions by spoilers); systems
  resist directed change (Jervis: interventions produce unintended
  nonlinear effects).
- **Assumptions:** adaptive capacity exceeds adversarial adaptation — an
  evolutionary race, not a fix.
- **Unintended consequences:** optimizing for current threats creates
  brittle specialization; new vulnerabilities from integration itself.
- **Durability:** the only candidate for *durable* transformation, and
  precisely the one least susceptible to engineered design — this
  tension is reported honestly.

## Deterrence vs. transformation (required distinction)

| Mechanism | Raises cost temporarily | Changes feasible set durably |
|---|---|---|
| A functional substitution | ✓ | if alternatives institutionalized |
| B opportunity cost | | ✓ while development persists |
| C dual-use | ✓ | ambiguous |
| D alignment | | ✓ if institutions self-enforcing |
| E pathway transformation | | ✓ strongest candidate |
| F adaptation | | ✓ but not designable |

Temporary deterrence moves utilities within a fixed structure;
transformation changes the structure. Only E/F qualify without caveat.
